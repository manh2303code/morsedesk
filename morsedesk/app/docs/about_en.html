What it works out on its own

MorseDesk measures the pitch, the speed and the alphabet from the recording itself. There is nothing to set before you decode.

Alphabet English, Russian or Kazakh. MorseDesk reads the message with each alphabet and keeps the one that reads like real language, then writes the whole message in that one alphabet. From about fifty characters on, the automatic choice is as good as choosing by hand.
Tone The pitch of the signal, anywhere from 300 to 1500 Hz. There is nothing to tune.
Speed Tested from 5 to 45 words per minute. Hand-sent Morse that speeds up and slows down is read too.
Confidence Words the program is unsure of are underlined: yellow means worth checking, red means probably wrong. Words without a line are sure. Click a word to hear that moment of the recording.

Strengths

Three alphabets in one decoder. Latin, Russian Cyrillic and Kazakh Cyrillic, chosen automatically, or by you.

Kazakh letters restored. Ә, Ғ, Қ, Ң, Ө, Ұ, Ү, Һ and І are sent with the codes of Russian letters, so the sound alone cannot tell Қ from К. The Kazakh language model decides which letter was meant: the greeting in the Kazakh sample, keyed as САЛЕМЕТСИЗ БЕ, is written СӘЛЕМЕТСІЗ БЕ.

Accurate in noise. On 500 generated test signals with noise, fading and interference, MorseDesk gets about 8 characters wrong in every 100. DeepCW, a public neural Morse decoder that reads Latin letters only, gets 24 wrong on the same signals. On ten real ARRL practice broadcasts (5 to 40 words per minute) about one character in a hundred was wrong, and the six sent at 18 to 40 words per minute (9,498 characters) came out exactly as published.

It does not make things up. When there is no Morse in the audio — silence, hiss, a steady carrier — you get an empty result and a message saying so, not invented letters. The one exception is a perfectly regular beeping, such as a buzzer: it has the rhythm of the letter E and is read as E.

Nothing leaves the computer. No internet connection, no account, no data collection. The program is a single file.

Getting started

When MorseDesk opens, a short Morse message saying hello in your interface language is already decoded on the Decode tab, so you can see how it works before opening your own file.

  1. Decode a recording. On the Decode tab, drop an audio file onto the window or click the folder button (Ctrl+O), then Decode. WAV, FLAC, OGG, MP3 and AIFF files up to one hour. The Signal panel shows the recording as a 3D landscape or a spectrogram.
  2. Check the result. The decoded text is shown in large letters. Press the round ▶ button (or click a word) to listen along: the word being played lights up and the text scrolls with it, like song lyrics. Copy the text, or export it as TXT or JSON with the buttons next to the player.
  3. Listen live. On the Listen tab, choose the sound input (a receiver connected to the line input, or a microphone), then Listen. Grey text may still change; plain text is final.
  4. Send Morse. On the Encode tab, type a message, then Play or Save…. Speed, tone, Farnsworth spacing and added noise are adjustable.
  5. Settings shows the model in use and the sound inputs found on this computer, and opens the log folder if something goes wrong.

As in Russian practice, Ё is sent as Е, and Ь and Ъ share one code (–··–). When decoding, the Kazakh language model chooses between the letters that share a code. If a message is too short to give context, the plain Russian letter may appear instead.

What is inside

A neural network with 1,119,425 parameters (4.5 MB) listens to the recording in 12-second windows and recognises whole Morse characters. It was trained only on generated signals, split evenly between English, Russian and Kazakh text, with noise, fading, interference and imperfect hand keying mixed in.

Three language models, one per language, then turn the characters into the most likely text. They are tables of how often sequences of up to six letters occur (English 19 MB, Russian 14 MB, Kazakh 10 MB).

A classic signal-processing decoder runs alongside. When the signal is very clean, its timing is more exact than the network's, and MorseDesk uses its reading instead.

The technical documentation describes every stage, with the measurements behind each design decision.

Limits you should know

  • Deep fading on a weak signal. When a weak signal fades below the noise, a third to half of the characters can be wrong. Letters that sank below the noise cannot be recovered by any decoder.
  • One signal at a time. MorseDesk follows the strongest tone. When two stations transmit at once, it decodes the stronger one.
  • Short messages. Automatic alphabet choice needs a few dozen characters; a short Russian message can look Kazakh, because the Kazakh alphabet contains every Russian letter. Radio abbreviations such as CQ, DE and 73 do not help: their codes exist in every alphabet (CQ becomes ЦЩ in Cyrillic). For short messages, choose the language yourself.
  • Live listening is a little less accurate than decoding a file, and trails the audio by one to two seconds. Once it has heard about fifty characters in one language, it stays with that language for the rest of the session.
  • Files up to one hour.

Computer requirements

System Windows 11 (64-bit), or Linux on x86-64. Tested on Windows 11, Ubuntu 22.04 and 24.04.
Processor Runs on the processor alone. No graphics card is needed.
Memory About 2 GB while decoding a one-hour recording. 8 GB of RAM or more is recommended.
Disk One file: about 140 MB on Windows, about 150 MB on Linux. On Windows it unpacks about 300 MB into a temporary folder while running and removes it on exit. The very first start of a new copy can take up to a minute while Windows scans the file.
Speed A one-hour recording takes about 9 minutes with automatic language choice, and about 3 minutes when you choose the language yourself (measured on an Intel Core Ultra 9 285H laptop processor).
Sound input Any input the system can record from, for live listening. Files need none.
