What it works out on its own

Most Morse decoders ask you to set the pitch, the speed and the alphabet before they can read anything. MorseDesk measures all three from the recording itself.

Alphabet English, Russian or Kazakh. MorseDesk reads the message with each alphabet and keeps the one that reads like real language. From about eight characters on, the automatic choice is as good as choosing by hand.
Tone The pitch of the signal, anywhere from 300 to 1500 Hz. There is nothing to tune.
Speed Tested from 5 to 45 words per minute, including hand-sent Morse that speeds up and slows down.
Confidence Every decoded word is coloured: green means sure, yellow means worth checking, red means probably wrong. Click a word to hear that moment of the recording.

Where it is stronger

Three alphabets in one decoder. Latin, Russian Cyrillic and Kazakh Cyrillic, chosen automatically, or by you.

Kazakh letters restored from context. Ә, Ғ, Қ, Ң, Ө, Ұ, Ү, Һ and І are sent with the codes of Russian letters, so the sound alone cannot tell Қ from К. A Kazakh language model decides from the surrounding words: a message keyed as САЛЕМ КАЗАКСТАН is written СӘЛЕМ ҚАЗАҚСТАН.

Accurate in noise. On our test set of 500 recordings with noise, fading and interference, MorseDesk gets about 8 characters wrong in every 100. DeepCW, a public neural Morse decoder, gets 24 wrong on the same recordings. On six real ARRL practice broadcasts sent at 18 to 40 words per minute (9,498 characters), the text came out exactly as published.

It does not make things up. When there is no Morse in the audio, you get an empty result and a message saying so, not invented letters.

Nothing leaves the computer. No internet connection, no account, no data collection. The program is a single file.

Getting started

  1. Decode a recording. On the Decode tab, drop an audio file onto the window or click Open file, then Decode. WAV, FLAC, OGG, MP3 and AIFF files up to one hour.
  2. Check the result. Words are coloured by confidence. Press Play to listen along; the word being played is outlined. Copy the text, or export it as TXT or JSON.
  3. Listen live. On the Listen tab, choose the sound input (a receiver connected to the line input, or a microphone), then Listen. Grey text may still change; plain text is final.
  4. Send Morse. On the Encode tab, type a message, then Play or Save. Speed, tone, Farnsworth spacing and added noise are adjustable.

As in Russian practice, Ё is sent as Е, and Ь and Ъ share one code (–··–). When decoding, the Kazakh language model chooses between the letters that share a code. If a message is too short to give context, the plain Russian letter may appear instead.

What is inside

A neural network with 1,119,425 parameters (4.5 MB) listens to the recording in 12-second windows and recognises whole Morse characters. It was trained only on generated signals, split evenly between English, Russian and Kazakh text, with noise, fading, interference and imperfect hand keying mixed in.

Three language models, one per language, then turn the characters into the most likely text. They are tables of how often sequences of up to six letters occur (English 19 MB, Russian 14 MB, Kazakh 10 MB).

A classic signal-processing decoder runs alongside. When the signal is very clean, its timing is more exact than the network's, and MorseDesk uses its reading instead.

The technical documentation describes every stage, with the measurements behind each design decision.

Limits you should know

  • Deep fading on a weak signal. When a weak signal fades below the noise, a third to more than half of the characters can be wrong. No decoder can recover letters that were never audible.
  • One signal at a time. MorseDesk follows the strongest tone. When two stations transmit at once, it decodes the stronger one.
  • Short messages. Automatic alphabet choice needs about eight characters. Radio abbreviations such as CQ, DE and 73 read the same in every alphabet; for those, choose the language yourself.
  • Live listening is about three characters in a hundred less accurate than decoding a file, and trails the audio by one to two seconds.
  • Files up to one hour.
  • The Kazakh interface is a draft that has not yet been reviewed by a native speaker.

Computer requirements

System Windows 10 or 11 (64-bit), or Linux on x86-64. Tested on Ubuntu 22.04 and 24.04.
Processor Runs on the processor alone. No graphics card is needed.
Memory About 2,033 MB while decoding a one-hour recording. 8 GB of RAM or more is recommended.
Disk One file of about 140 MB. On Windows it unpacks about 300 MB into a temporary folder while running and removes it on exit.
Speed A one-hour recording takes about 9 minutes with automatic language choice, and about 3 minutes when you choose the language yourself (measured on an Intel Core Ultra 9 285H laptop processor).
Sound input Any input the system can record from, for live listening. Files need none.
