MorseDesk technical reference

Application 0.1.0, acoustic model 0.4.0. Measurements dated 23 September 2026 unless noted.

MorseDesk turns Morse code audio into text in English, Russian and Kazakh, and text back into Morse audio. It runs entirely offline on the processor. This document describes every processing stage, the numbers each stage was chosen by, and what the system cannot do.

Colour marks the processing layer throughout the document: signal, acoustic model, language, classic decoder and path selection, confidence and safety.

Pipeline at a glance

Stages run in this order for every file. Times are for one 15.8-second clip on an Intel Core Ultra 9 285H laptop processor, with the language chosen automatically.

StageWhat happensTime
1. Load Decode the file, mix to mono, resample to 8 kHz. 
2. Find the tone Locate the Morse pitch between 300 and 1500 Hz; estimate signal-to-noise ratio. 14 ms
3. Features Add calibrated noise to very clean audio; compute 11 features per 5 ms in 12-second windows.76 ms
4. Acoustic model Neural network outputs, every 10 ms, the probability of each of 65 symbols. 87 ms
5. Beam search and language model Search for the most likely text under each language; choose the language. 3,307 ms
6. Classic decoder An independent threshold-and-timing decoder reads the same signal. 43 ms
7. Choose a path Keep the classic reading only when its timing evidence is strong and it is complete.  
8. Check confidence Return empty with a warning rather than text the model barely believes.  

The result carries the text, each word with its start and end time and a confidence between 0 and 1, the measured speed (WPM), tone (Hz), signal-to-noise ratio (dB), the chosen language with its score for every language, which path produced the text, and any warnings.

Where the time goes. Beam search with the language model takes about 91 % of the decoding time; the neural network takes about 2.4 %. This is why choosing the language by hand, which searches one language instead of three, decodes two and a half to three times faster.

Signal layer

Loading audio

WAV, FLAC, OGG/OGA, AIFF/AIF and MP3 are read through the libsndfile library bundled with the program. Stereo is averaged to mono. Everything is resampled to 8,000 samples per second with a polyphase filter (resample_poly); simple linear interpolation is not used, because it measurably raises the character error rate. Files longer than one hour are refused.

Finding the tone

A short-time Fourier transform (256-point Hann window, 31.25 Hz per bin, a frame every 5 ms) gives the power of each frequency over time. For each frequency, MorseDesk takes the 90th percentile of power over time: a keyed carrier is on often enough to stand out there, while noise is not. The tone is the highest peak between 300 and 1500 Hz that rises at least 6 dB above the median of that band. Parabolic interpolation over the three bins around the peak refines the frequency to well below one bin.

The signal-to-noise ratio is reported in a 500 Hz bandwidth, the convention of amateur radio. Noise is the median power of bins four to eight bins away from the tone; the figure is corrected for the spread of the Hann window (1.76 dB) and the band width (12.04 dB). A second peak at least 6 dB above the band and at least half the strength of the first raises the warning second tone.

On long files the analysis uses at most 60 seconds, taken as six slices spread evenly across the recording. For a 20-minute file this step needs about 92 MB instead of 1.8 GB, with the same answer.

When no peak clears 6 dB. Under deep fading at 0 dB, a real signal often fails the test. MorseDesk then takes the strongest bin anyway and marks the result guessed tone. This rescues about 39 of every 500 test clips (worth 3.5 points of error rate). The guess is refused when the band holds no energy at all, so digital silence stays silent.

Dither for very clean audio

The network was trained on signal-to-noise ratios from −6 to 25 dB. Real broadcast recordings measure 49 to 143 dB, a condition it never saw. Above 25 dB MorseDesk therefore adds white noise with a fixed seed to bring the signal down to 20 dB. On a 20 WPM ARRL recording this alone moved the network from 89.8 % to 29.9 % error (measured with an earlier model). On such clean recordings the classic decoder (below) usually supplies the segmentation.

Eleven features every 5 milliseconds

ColumnContent
0Envelope: the audio is mixed down to 0 Hz at the tone, low-pass filtered at 200 Hz (zero-phase), its magnitude averaged over each 40-sample frame, then converted to a logarithm and normalised by automatic gain control: subtract a running mean, divide by a running standard deviation (time constant 1 s, started from the first 0.5 s), clip to ±6.
1Rate of change of column 0, which marks key-down and key-up edges.
2–10Log power in nine spectral bins centred on the tone (±125 Hz), normalised by their median and 95th percentile. They let the network see an interfering station next to the wanted one.

Twelve-second windows

Features are computed in 12-second windows every 2 seconds. Each window finds its own tone and normalises its own level, so drifting pitch and changing signal strength are followed. A window without a detectable tone reuses the previous window's tone. The network runs on each window; for every frame, the output of the window whose centre is nearest is kept. Where a window that carries keyed Morse meets its neighbour, 0.5 s of noise at the recording's own background level is added before the network runs, and the matching output is dropped: a window cut from the middle of a long message is full of signal from edge to edge, unlike any training clip, and without this margin the network returned nothing at all for about four windows in ten (a 2-minute message: 50 % character error, 1.2 % with the margin). The two ends of the recording are left as they are, and so are windows with no tone or with a steady unkeyed carrier: there the quiet margin would give the level control a calm reference and turn clicks or a carrier into letters.

Acoustic model

A small convolutional-recurrent network trained with connectionist temporal classification (CTC). It is stored as ONNX (opset 17, 4.5 MB) and runs with ONNX Runtime on the processor; no graphics card and no deep-learning framework is needed at run time.

65 symbols: whole Morse characters, not letters

The network does not output letters. It outputs one of 62 Morse patterns (for example ·– or –···), a word space, an unknown mark, or the CTC blank. The same pattern means A in English, А in Russian, and А or Ә in Kazakh; turning patterns into letters is the language layer's job. This is why a single network serves all three alphabets, and why the alphabet can be decided after listening rather than before.

Training

All training audio is generated on the fly and never repeated: the production model saw 60,000 batches of 16 clips of 4 to 12 seconds, about 960,000 clips or roughly 2,100 hours of Morse. Languages are drawn in equal thirds (measured 33.3 % each).

Text60 % strings of common words from a built-in list (51 English, 40 Russian, 40 Kazakh words, chosen so that every letter occurs), 30 % amateur-radio exchanges with random call signs, 10 % random character groups. How this model was chosen

Candidates are compared by character error rate on fixed test sets, all decoded with the same pipeline — never by the validation score printed during training, because each training run draws its own validation clips and two runs would be graded on different exams. Of the variations tried on the earlier, smaller network (heavier fading, per-window features, longer training clips, training on 12-second windows, extra very clean signals), only a larger network helped clearly; the others were no better or worse.

The current model (0.4.0)

Model 0.4.0 makes the network larger again (hidden size 384, 1,119,425 parameters) and trains it on harder fading for 60,000 steps. It was compared with model 0.3.0 on the same clips, with the same pipeline, and adopted because it won on the development set, the win held on T1, and it cost nothing on real recordings.

Language layer

Beam search

A CTC prefix beam search keeps the 64 best partial transcripts. Each Morse pattern expands into every letter it can mean in the current alphabet, and each candidate is scored as

acoustic log-probability + 0.3 × language-model log-probability + 1.0 per word + 1.25 per character + 1.0 per known word

The per-character bonus exists because 74 % of the network's errors were deletions, not insertions; without it the search was too timid on fading signals. Known words are common radio words for English (CQ, DE, PSE, RST, QTH, 73 and others) and a few common words for Russian and Kazakh (ПРИВЕТ, СПАСИБО, СӘЛЕМ, РАХМЕТ, ҚАЗАҚСТАН). Symbols with probability below 0.001 in a frame are skipped, with at most eight per frame. The weights were tuned for model 0.3.0 (33 configurations on a separate development set, none significantly better) and kept for 0.4.0.

Choosing the language

The network output is split at silences of at least 3 seconds, and at most every 30 seconds. For each segment, beam search runs once per language; the score is the language-model log-probability per character of the best transcript. The highest score wins. When the lead is under 0.15 nats per character, the segment keeps the previous segment's language. The file's language is the vote of all segments weighted by their character counts, and the whole message is then written in that one alphabet: a real message does not switch language part-way, so a short noisy segment that happens to score better in another language no longer comes out in another alphabet. Every segment has already been searched in every language, so this costs nothing.

Classic decoder and path selection

The classic decoder

It reads the envelope with a Schmitt trigger (the off threshold is 0.67 of the on threshold, so noise near the threshold cannot chatter) at six thresholds from 0.15 to 0.65 of the signal range. Pulses shorter than 0.6 of a dit are removed. The dit length is estimated by clustering the logarithms of pulse lengths into two groups, and every duration is compared with the ideal proportions: 1 and 3 for dits and dahs, 1, 3 and 7 for the gaps. The threshold whose timing fits best wins; a weak preference for 20 WPM only breaks ties. Its timing cost measures how far the durations sit from the ideal grid.

On noisy clips it is far worse than the network (35.12 % error on T1 on its own). On clean real recordings it is better, because its timing is exact.

When the classic reading is kept

The classic decoder runs on every clip. Its reading replaces the network's only when all of these hold:

  1. Every pattern it read exists in the alphabet.
  2. Either its timing cost is below 0.02, or the signal-to-noise ratio is above 30 dB (outside the training range) and the timing cost is below 0.06.
  3. Its text is at least 0.9 times as long as the network's.

Even then, letters are still chosen by the language model: the classic decoder supplies the segmentation into patterns, and the language model decides between А and Ә. The network's reading is kept in the result as an alternative.

Why the length rule exists. On a fading signal the classic decoder reads the strong parts perfectly and leaves the faded parts blank; what it did read fits the grid, so its timing cost stays low, and rule 2 alone would accept it. Without rule 3, clips at 20 dB with fading that took the classic path had 72.5 % error against 11.5 % for the network; with it, fading at 20 dB is 5.32 % on T1 and 6.75 % on the development set instead of 14.18 % and 42.00 %. Dropping rule 2's timing branch altogether scores better on generated clips but more than triples the error on the real recordings, so the branch stays.

Confidence and safety

Word confidence

Each word's confidence comes from the network's probabilities for the frames of its characters. The interface underlines words below 0.8 in yellow and below 0.5 in red; words from 0.8 up carry no mark. When the playback runs, the word being played is highlighted. When the average over the clip is below 0.6 the result carries the warning low confidence; below 0.5, the classic decoder's reading is shown next to it.

No invented text

A network trained only on Morse has never been taught to say "there is nothing here", and the language model will shape noise into plausible words. Left unchecked, it does: in a test of 81 inputs without Morse, digital silence became T, a steady carrier 73, white noise SEE. Two rules prevent it:

  • no tone is guessed from a band that holds no energy;
  • when the average confidence over the whole clip is below 0.15, the result is empty, with the warning no Morse signal, while tone and signal-to-noise are still reported.

In 120 runs of noise without Morse, the result is empty every time. On test clips that do contain Morse, the threshold removed three clips on T1 (which were already more than 70 % wrong) and none on the development set. One case remains: noise that switches on and off perfectly regularly is, by its envelope, a Morse string of E, and comes out as EEEE.

No network access

The application code imports no networking library, and a test blocks every socket and then runs a full encode and decode. The only socket in the Windows build is the loopback connection (127.0.0.1) the PyInstaller launcher uses to close its splash screen.

Live listening

Audio from the sound card is captured at 8 kHz. Every 0.5 s the live decoder runs the same decoder as for files on a sliding window holding the last 8 s of context, and trusts only text that is at least 1 s old, so the network has heard what comes after it. Words are committed by their absolute time in the stream, so repeated words such as CQ CQ are not merged. Windows without a tone are treated as silence, which stops the network reading background noise as letters.

With the language set to automatic, each decode sees only the last few seconds. Every decode also scores the text with all three language models, and these scores are added up over the whole session, each stretch of audio counted once. Once about fifty characters have been committed and one language leads the next by a clear margin, the session keeps that language until it is cleared, so the running text never switches alphabet part-way. An earlier rule (three decodes in a row agreeing) looked at only about 30 characters and locked the wrong language in 26 of 54 two-minute test messages.

The Kazakh alphabet is the Russian table plus nine letters, each sent with the code of the closest Russian letter. Fading by signal strength

Nearly all remaining error is fading. In T1 the fade is 20 dB deep with a 4-second period, so at 20 dB the troughs reach 0 dB, which the decoder handles on a steady signal (clean at 0 dB: about 3 %). At 6 dB and below the troughs fall 14 to 20 dB under the noise, and most letters there cannot be recovered.

How precise these numbers are

By bootstrap, the overall error rate of a 500-clip set is known to about ±1 point, a 100-clip channel to ±0.5–2.8 points, and a 20-clip cell to ±7 points. Two configurations are therefore compared on the same clips (paired), where the uncertainty of the difference is about 0.2 points. When many configurations are tried, the best one is partly luck: of 33 language-model settings, the best gained 0.34 points on the development set and 0.01 on T1.

Limits and known issues

  • Deep fading at 6 dB and below: 32 to 59 % character error. The troughs sink below the noise; this is mostly a physical limit.
  • One signal: the strongest tone is decoded; a second station of similar strength raises a warning.
  • Short messages: the automatic alphabet choice is reliable from about 50 characters. Radio abbreviations do not help: their codes exist in every alphabet (CQ becomes ЦЩ in Cyrillic).
  • One alphabet per message: a message that really mixes languages is written entirely in the language that dominates it.
  • Live mode: about 3 points less accurate than files (model 0.3.0); 1.3 s behind the audio.
  • Periodic noise can be read as a string of E.
  • Kazakh: when context is short, the Russian letter may appear where a Kazakh letter was meant (К for Қ).
  • Files up to one hour. Memory grows with the length of the file: about 2 GB for one hour of audio, because the network's output for all overlapping windows is kept until the end.
